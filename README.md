CS-255 System Analysis and Design Portfolio

Portfolio Overview

This repository contains two major artifacts from my CS-255 System Analysis and Design course at Southern New Hampshire University: the DriverPass Business Requirements Document from Project One and the DriverPass System Design Document from Project Two. Together, these documents demonstrate how I analyzed a client's business needs, translated those needs into system requirements, and used UML models and technical design descriptions to communicate a proposed solution.

1. DriverPass Project Summary

DriverPass was the client for this project. The company wanted to help customers prepare for driving tests administered by the Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) by combining online learning and practice tests with on-the-road driving instruction. The proposed system needed to support customer registration, training-package selection, two-hour driving lesson scheduling, appointment changes and cancellations, and the management of customer, driver, and vehicle information. It also needed to track practice-test progress, support employee roles and permissions, maintain reservation activity records, generate reports, and support updates to DMV rules, policies, and sample questions. My role in the project was to analyze these business needs and develop a system design that represented how the proposed web-based solution would support customer services and DriverPass's internal operations.

2. What I Did Particularly Well

I believe I did well at connecting the client's business requirements to the proposed system design. In Project One, I organized DriverPass's needs into functional and nonfunctional requirements, user-interface requirements, objectives, assumptions, and limitations. I also included a Gantt chart to represent the project schedule. In Project Two, I used five UML diagrams to communicate different aspects of the proposed system: a use case diagram for user interactions, activity diagrams for driving lesson scheduling and online practice testing, a sequence diagram for scheduling interactions, and a class diagram for the system's information structures and relationships. The scheduling diagrams are particularly useful because they represent important business rules, including checking driver and vehicle availability, handling unavailable appointment times, and recording reservation activity. Together, these artifacts show how requirements analysis can guide a structured system design and give technical team members different views of the same proposed solution.

3. What I Would Revise and How I Would Improve It

If I could revise one aspect of my work, I would strengthen the traceability between individual business requirements, the UML diagrams, and the tests needed to verify the system. I would create a requirements traceability matrix linking each important requirement to the relevant design elements and corresponding test scenarios. For example, I could map the two-hour lesson requirement, driver and vehicle availability checks, matching pickup and drop-off locations, Package Three practice-test eligibility, role-based access, and reservation activity logging to the appropriate diagrams and tests. I would then review those connections to identify any requirement that lacks a clear design representation or verification method. This improvement would make it easier to confirm that the design addresses the client's documented needs and would help developers and testers understand how each requirement should be implemented and evaluated.

4. How I Interpreted User Needs and Incorporated Them Into the Design

I interpreted DriverPass's needs by examining the project scenario, identifying the different user roles, and determining what each user needed to accomplish. Customers needed to register, select training packages, manage driving appointments, and access online learning features when eligible. The secretary needed to assist customers with scheduling, while the owner and IT officer needed access to business information and administrative functions appropriate to their responsibilities. I translated these needs into system requirements and used UML diagrams to represent user interactions, workflows, and information relationships. For example, the driving lesson activity diagram represents availability checking and the handling of unavailable appointment times, while the practice-test activity diagram represents package eligibility and test-progress tracking. Considering user needs is important because a system can be technically well organized but still fail if it does not support the tasks users actually need to perform. Understanding user roles, business rules, and alternative scenarios helps keep the design aligned with the client's objectives and reduces the risk of developing functionality that does not solve the underlying problem.

5. My Approach to Software Design and Strategies for the Future

My approach to software design is to understand the problem and stakeholders' needs before deciding how the system should be implemented. I would begin by reviewing the project scenario, gathering requirements, identifying user roles, and distinguishing functional requirements from nonfunctional requirements. I would document assumptions, limitations, and unresolved questions so that unconfirmed technical decisions are not mistaken for established requirements. Next, I would use appropriate modeling techniques to communicate different perspectives of the system. Use case diagrams help show interactions between users and the system, activity diagrams clarify workflows and decision paths, sequence diagrams explain interactions over time, and class diagrams represent structural elements and their relationships. In future projects, I would create a requirements traceability matrix, review the models with stakeholders, consider unsuccessful and exceptional scenarios, and derive test cases from the requirements. Finally, I would keep the documentation consistent and update it when requirements change. These strategies would help identify gaps earlier, improve communication between stakeholders and technical teams, and support implementation, testing, and future maintenance.
